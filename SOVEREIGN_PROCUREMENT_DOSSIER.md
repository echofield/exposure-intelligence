## Section 1 - Executive Verdict

### The Strategic Finding

The target is not facing an "AI governance" problem. It is facing a procurement admissibility problem.

Its commercial upside depends on its ability to prove four things quickly and without improvisation:

1. Sensitive data is not exposed to unacceptable jurisdictional or operational control.
2. Critical workflows can survive vendor exit, migration, service disruption, and supervisory review.
3. AI roles, intended use, model dependencies, and human oversight are documented before the buyer asks.
4. The buyer can award, onboard, and defend the procurement decision without becoming the target's compliance department.

If the target cannot produce this evidence, sophisticated buyers will not simply ask for "more AI governance." They will delay, downscope, demand price concessions, require remediation before signature, or select a safer bidder.

### Brutal Read

The target's product may be good. Its procurement posture is not yet bankable.

The current weak structure is typical: promising sovereignty language, scattered cloud diagrams, standard vendor terms, generic security claims, incomplete sub-processor disclosure, no tested exit playbook, and product documentation that does not line up cleanly with AI Act role boundaries.

That is not a documentation nuisance. It is revenue leakage.

### Commercial Exposure Profile

**Overall profile:** High

**Why high:** The target is trying to sell into markets where procurement teams do not buy software features alone. They buy defendability. The target's ability to win depends on whether its evidence pack lets a public buyer, bank, hospital, insurer, or strategic operator say: "This vendor can pass legal, security, operational resilience, data protection, and internal audit review without creating unmanaged exposure for us."

### Top Five Deal-Killers

| Deal-killer | Why it blocks procurement | Current evidence posture |
|---|---|---|
| Sovereignty claim without proof | Public and health buyers cannot rely on marketing language for sensitive data processing | No single jurisdictional control dossier |
| No tested reversibility plan | Financial buyers cannot onboard critical ICT services without credible exit and continuity evidence | Generic export promises only |
| Opaque model dependency | Buyer cannot know which model, API, sub-processor, or infrastructure layer touches sensitive data | Partial architecture diagrams, no evidence chain |
| Misaligned intended use | Sales language implies decision support in regulated contexts while terms disclaim responsibility | High reclassification and procurement friction |
| Weak sub-outsourcing control | Buyer cannot register or approve the dependency chain cleanly | Dynamic vendor list, limited auditability |

### Partner-Level Interpretation

This dossier gives counsel a premium engagement wedge:

- Turn vague "AI risk" into concrete procurement blockers.
- Convert blockers into contract terms, evidence requests, and technical remediation.
- Help the client become easier to buy, easier to defend, and harder to replace.
- Price the work as market-access structuring, not compliance hygiene.

## Section 3 - Procurement Gate Map

### Gate 1: Sovereignty And Sensitive Data

French public-sector and health-adjacent procurement is increasingly sensitive to where data is processed, who controls the infrastructure, who can access support systems, and whether a foreign legal regime can reach the data or keys.

The French SREN framework and its 2026 implementing materials around particularly sensitive state data create a strong buyer expectation around qualified cloud services and protection from extraterritorial exposure. Source anchor: [French Ministry of Economy note on Article 31 SREN decree](https://www.economie.gouv.fr/daj/publication-du-decret-dapplication-de-larticle-31-de-la-loi-sren-relatif-la-protection-des-donnees-dune-sensibilite-particuliere-de-letat).

**Procurement implication:** A vendor that cannot explain hosting, support access, key control, sub-processors, and jurisdictional exposure in one coherent pack forces the buyer to do the vendor's work.

### Gate 2: Operational Resilience And Reversibility

Financial-sector buyers subject to DORA must manage ICT third-party risk, concentration risk, contractual controls, and exit strategies for critical or important functions. Source anchor: [EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj).

**Procurement implication:** A vendor that offers only "data export on termination" is not procurement-ready for serious financial buyers. The buyer needs a tested transition story: data, configurations, logs, dependencies, service continuity, costs, timeframes, and fallback operations.

### Gate 3: AI Act Role And Intended Use

The EU AI Act creates different obligations depending on the role of the actor and the risk category of the system. The Commission has announced timeline changes for high-risk rules in areas such as employment, education, migration, and critical infrastructure, with 2 December 2027 identified for stand-alone high-risk systems in the 2026 simplification package. Source anchor: [European Commission AI Act simplification announcement](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/eu-agrees-simplify-ai-rules-boost-innovation-and-ban-nudification-apps-protect-citizens).

**Procurement implication:** Buyers will ask whether the vendor is a provider, deployer, distributor, importer, model modifier, or downstream integrator. If product architecture, sales decks, terms, and documentation are inconsistent, procurement friction increases.

### Gate 4: Data Protection And Sub-Processing

GDPR review remains central where customer data, employee data, health data, public-service records, or user prompts are processed. Buyer review focuses on lawful processing basis, processor/controller roles, international transfers, sub-processors, deletion, retention, access logs, and security measures.

**Procurement implication:** A DPA that covers generic SaaS processing may still fail to answer the buyer's AI-specific questions: what data reaches the model layer, what is retained, what is logged, who can inspect it, and whether it is used for model improvement.

### Gate 5: Product Liability And Digital Product Risk

EU product liability rules have been adapted to explicitly cover software, AI systems, and related digital services. Source anchor: [European Commission product liability update](https://commission.europa.eu/news/eu-adapts-product-liability-rules-digital-age-and-circular-economy-2024-12-09_en).

**Procurement implication:** Buyers will not only ask whether the AI is "compliant." They will ask who carries responsibility for defective outputs, unsafe workflow design, insufficient warnings, unavailable logs, or model behavior that cannot be reconstructed.

### Gate 6: Strategic And Green Public Procurement

For public works and infrastructure-adjacent procurement, sustainability evidence, lifecycle data, traceability, and procurement transparency can affect bid defensibility. Source anchor: [OECD strategic and green public procurement in France](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/promoting-strategic-and-green-public-procurement-in-france_70da2048-en.html).

**Procurement implication:** Where the target supports construction, infrastructure, public works, energy, health, transport, or supplier-management workflows, its evidence layer can become part of the buyer's award narrative.

## Section 4 - The Procurement Kill-Chain

This section maps how a promising deal dies.

| Stage | What the vendor thinks is happening | What the buyer is actually testing | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demo | Buyer is evaluating features | Buyer is identifying risk owners | Vendor cannot name which evidence belongs to legal, security, DPO, procurement, or operational resilience |
| Security review | Buyer is checking controls | Buyer is looking for undocumented access paths | Cloud, support, model, and sub-processor evidence is fragmented |
| Legal review | Buyer is negotiating terms | Buyer is testing whether contractual promises match architecture | MSA, DPA, AI terms, and architecture diagrams contradict each other |
| Data protection review | Buyer is checking GDPR posture | Buyer is asking what data reaches model layers and where it goes | No model-layer data-flow map |
| Financial resilience review | Buyer is checking exit | Buyer is asking how to operate if the vendor fails | No tested reversibility playbook |
| Internal audit | Buyer is checking defensibility | Buyer is asking whether the decision can be defended later | Vendor evidence cannot be reused in audit or board materials |
| Award or signature | Vendor expects close | Buyer reopens exceptions | Deal slows, scope shrinks, price drops, or competitor wins |

### Brutal Finding

Most vendors lose regulated deals before they know they are losing them.

The buyer does not say: "Your sovereignty posture is commercially non-bankable."

The buyer says: "Legal is still reviewing," "security has follow-up questions," "risk wants a workshop," or "we need to align internally."

Those phrases are usually evidence failure in polite language.

## Section 5 - Evidence Architecture Required

### The Minimum Bankable Evidence Pack

| Evidence asset | Buyer question it answers | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Sovereignty statement | Why can this vendor process sensitive data without unacceptable jurisdictional exposure? | Counsel + CTO + DPO |
| Hosting and key-control map | Where is data stored, processed, encrypted, and accessed? | CTO + security |
| Model-layer data-flow map | What reaches the model, what is retained, and what is excluded? | AI lead + security |
| Sub-processor register | Who can touch the data, infrastructure, logs, or support systems? | Legal + vendor management |
| Support access procedure | Can vendor staff or third parties access customer environments? | Security + operations |
| DORA reversibility playbook | How does the buyer exit without operational rupture? | CTO + counsel |
| AI Act role matrix | Which actor carries which obligation across product layers? | Counsel + product |
| Intended-use alignment matrix | Do product docs, demos, sales decks, terms, and implementation guides say the same thing? | Product + legal |
| Audit log specification | Can material actions and AI outputs be reconstructed? | Engineering |
| Procurement response pack | Can buyer teams reuse the evidence internally without rewriting it? | Legal + sales engineering |

### What Makes The Pack Premium

The pack is not a folder of policies. It is a buyer decision instrument.

Each section must be written in a form that a buyer's legal, security, DPO, procurement, financial-risk, and audit teams can reuse directly. The artifact is premium when it reduces the buyer's internal workload.

### The Standard Failure

The vendor gives each buyer a different answer depending on which department asks the question.

Security receives a diagram. Legal receives a DPA. Procurement receives a questionnaire. The DPO receives a sub-processor page. The bank's third-party risk team receives an export clause. None of these artifacts line up.

The result is not "incomplete compliance." The result is a loss of buyer confidence.

## Section 6 - Sovereignty Control Matrix

### Control Questions

| Control question | Strong answer | Weak answer | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where is sensitive data hosted? | Specific provider, region, service boundary, and certification posture | "Hosted in Europe" | Hosting map and provider evidence |
| Who controls encryption keys? | Buyer-controlled or sovereignty-aligned key control with documented access path | Provider-managed keys with broad support access | Key-management architecture |
| Can non-EU personnel access data or logs? | Access restricted, logged, approved, and contractually bounded | Support access handled by global team | Support access procedure |
| Are AI prompts and outputs retained? | Retention period, purpose, storage location, deletion flow documented | "We do not train on your data" only | Model-layer retention schedule |
| Are model providers sub-processors? | Named in register with data categories and safeguards | Not listed or described as "infrastructure" | Sub-processor register |
| Are inference logs treated as customer data? | Yes, with retention, access, deletion, and export controls | Not separately addressed | Log data classification |
| Can the buyer exclude sensitive categories from model processing? | Technical controls and configuration guide exist | Handled through user policy only | Data-minimization controls |

### Red Flags

- The vendor says "sovereign" but uses a foreign-controlled support chain with unclear access rights.
- The vendor says "EU hosted" but cannot distinguish storage, processing, support, backup, logs, telemetry, and model inference.
- The vendor says "no training" but cannot explain prompt retention, abuse monitoring, embeddings storage, or inference logging.
- The vendor says "SecNumCloud-ready" but cannot show which layer is actually hosted on qualified infrastructure.
- The vendor's architecture diagram has no legal entity, sub-processor, or access-control overlay.

### Procurement-Grade Output

The dossier should produce a one-page sovereignty statement that answers:

1. What data categories are processed.
2. Which infrastructure services process them.
3. Which legal entities can access them.
4. Which personnel categories can access them.
5. Which jurisdictions are implicated.
6. Which keys protect the data.
7. Which logs are generated.
8. Which contractual controls bind the access chain.
9. Which residual questions require legal interpretation.

## Section 7 - DORA Reversibility Architecture

### Why Generic Exit Terms Fail

"Customer may export data upon termination" is not a reversibility plan.

A regulated financial buyer needs to know whether it can continue critical or important functions if the vendor degrades, fails, is terminated, becomes non-compliant, changes sub-processors, or is ordered to exit.

### Reversibility Components

| Component | Required evidence | Procurement value |
|---|---|---|
| Data export | Schema, format, frequency, completeness, test results | Buyer can assess migration feasibility |
| Configuration export | Rules, prompts, workflows, user roles, templates | Buyer can rebuild operating context |
| Log export | Audit logs, AI outputs, user actions, system events | Buyer can preserve traceability |
| Dependency map | Cloud, model, database, identity, telemetry, support | Buyer can identify concentration risk |
| Migration runbook | Steps, owners, timing, success criteria | Buyer can review operational continuity |
| Alternative environment | Sovereign cloud, private cloud, on-premise, or escrowed package | Buyer can assess fallback realism |
| Contract schedule | Exit assistance, fees, service levels, cooperation duties | Buyer can price and enforce exit |
| Test certificate | Evidence of a dry-run or tabletop exercise | Buyer can defend reliance internally |

### Exit Scenarios To Model

| Scenario | Required response |
|---|---|
| Vendor voluntary termination | Data, configuration, and log export within defined period |
| Buyer termination for cause | Accelerated exit support and transition cooperation |
| Cloud provider failure | Secondary deployment option or documented restoration path |
| Model provider withdrawal | Model substitution process and regression-testing method |
| Regulatory objection | Rapid isolation, suspension, or migration of affected processing |
| Security incident | Evidence preservation, access lockdown, and clean-room migration |
| Change of control | Buyer termination or additional assurance rights |

### Brutal Finding

The target's reversibility posture is only real if it has been tested.

A beautiful exit clause without a tested export is a procurement decoration.

## Section 8 - AI Role And Intended-Use Matrix

### Role Discipline

The target must be able to explain its role by layer. It should not answer role questions at company level only.

| Layer | Possible role question | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation model | Is the target the model provider, modifier, distributor, or deployer? | Model source, license, modification record |
| Orchestration layer | Does the target determine intended purpose and system behavior? | Product architecture and intended-use docs |
| Customer configuration | Can customer configuration shift the system into high-risk use? | Implementation guide and restricted-use controls |
| Output use | Are outputs used for decisions about persons, access, benefits, employment, credit, or services? | Use-case register and human oversight matrix |
| Monitoring | Are logs sufficient to reconstruct material decisions? | Logging specification |
| Marketing | Do sales decks imply a broader use than terms allow? | Marketing alignment matrix |

### Intended-Use Misalignment Test

| Document | Question |
|---|---|
| Website | Does it imply autonomous decision-making or regulated decision support? |
| Sales deck | Does it promise replacement of human review? |
| Demo script | Does it show use in HR, health, financial eligibility, public benefits, or critical operations? |
| MSA | Does it disclaim the same uses the sales process encourages? |
| Product docs | Do configuration examples create foreseeable high-risk use? |
| Implementation guide | Are restricted uses technically blocked or merely prohibited in wording? |
| Support docs | Can support staff guide customers into risky configurations? |

### High-Risk Watchlist

The following use categories require immediate counsel review before being used in a procurement response:

- Recruitment, candidate screening, employee evaluation, worker monitoring, or task allocation
- Creditworthiness, loan eligibility, fraud scoring, insurance eligibility, or customer risk classification
- Access to essential public or private services
- Healthcare triage, clinical prioritization, diagnosis, or treatment recommendation
- Public administration decision support affecting individuals
- Education admission, progression, or evaluation
- Critical infrastructure operation or safety decision support

### Brutal Finding

The target does not control its AI Act posture only through its terms.

It controls it through the combined evidence of product behavior, product docs, sales language, implementation examples, customer configuration, support practice, and actual output use.

If those materials are inconsistent, a buyer will price the inconsistency as risk.

## Section 9 - Contract Architecture

### Clauses That Matter Commercially

| Clause area | Weak form | Strong form |
|---|---|---|
| Data processing | Generic DPA attached to MSA | AI-specific processing schedule with model, log, prompt, output, telemetry, and sub-processor treatment |
| Sub-processing | Vendor may update list online | Notice period, objection right, critical sub-processor classification, and impact statement |
| Model changes | Vendor may improve service at discretion | Material model-change notice, regression testing, and buyer opt-out for regulated workflows |
| Support access | Standard support rights | Named access classes, approval workflow, logging, emergency access rules |
| Reversibility | Export on termination | Tested exit plan, transition assistance, export schemas, configuration portability |
| Audit | Security report only | Audit evidence pack, regulator cooperation, buyer questionnaire support |
| AI outputs | Customer responsible for all use | Shared evidence duties, output traceability, disclaimers matched to product behavior |
| Restricted use | Boilerplate prohibition | Technical controls, configuration flags, implementation review, and escalation |
| Change of control | Silent | Buyer notice and termination rights where sovereignty posture changes |
| Incident response | Generic security incident notice | AI-specific incident categories: output defect, model drift, unauthorized prompt retention, sub-processor exposure |

### Downstream Procurement Clauses

The target should prepare buyer-ready language covering:

- data residency and hosting boundary
- support access restrictions
- key-management responsibilities
- AI data processing schedule
- sub-processor notification and objection
- model-change notification
- audit and evidence cooperation
- reversibility and exit support
- public-sector procurement evidence cooperation
- regulator and supervisory authority cooperation
- prohibited and restricted use boundaries

### The Negotiation Lever

Strong contract architecture is not defensive paperwork. It is a sales accelerator.

The target with pre-drafted, evidence-backed clauses can move faster than competitors that negotiate each regulated buyer from zero.

## Section 12 - Remediation Roadmap

### First 10 Days

- Freeze all sovereignty, security, AI, and procurement claims until they are mapped to evidence.
- Create a single owner for the procurement evidence pack.
- Build a complete AI and infrastructure dependency inventory.
- Identify every sub-processor touching customer data, prompts, logs, outputs, telemetry, or support systems.
- Collect existing contracts, DPAs, security schedules, cloud diagrams, AI terms, and product docs.
- Mark contradictions between product claims, architecture, and legal terms.

### Days 10-30

- Draft the sovereignty statement.
- Build the model-layer data-flow map.
- Build the sub-processor register with AI-specific data categories.
- Draft the intended-use alignment matrix.
- Draft the restricted-use and high-risk escalation guide.
- Draft the first DORA reversibility playbook.
- Identify contract clauses that must be upgraded before regulated procurement.

### Days 30-60

- Run a tabletop exit exercise.
- Test data export, configuration export, and log export.
- Validate support-access and emergency-access procedures.
- Prepare standard buyer responses for public-sector, health, and financial procurement.
- Redraft procurement-facing sales materials so claims and terms align.
- Create a deal-desk trigger: no regulated buyer proposal goes out without the procurement evidence pack attached.

### Days 60-90

- Negotiate upgraded sub-processor and model-provider commitments where possible.
- Package the procurement dossier for board review.
- Build a regulated-buyer onboarding data room.
- Train sales engineering and legal teams on the buyer objection map.
- Build renewal and change-control cadence for the evidence pack.
- Decide whether the architecture should support a sovereign deployment tier.

## Section 14 - Board Summary

### What The Board Needs To Know

The target's ability to sell into regulated European buyers depends less on AI features than on procurement defensibility.

1. sovereignty claims are not supported by a single evidence chain
